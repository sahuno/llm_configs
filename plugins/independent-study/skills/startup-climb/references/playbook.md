# Stage playbook

Plain-language text for each camp's "In the general playbook" panel. Adapt the wording to the
company (one or two sentences, no jargon without a short definition). Pick the entry that matches
what the round actually was, not just its letter: a "Series B" that was really a rescue round
should use the down-round entry.

| Stage | Playbook text (starting point) |
|---|---|
| Bootstrapped / friends & family | Founders fund the first version themselves or with help from people they know, usually well under $1M. |
| Pre-seed / seed | A few million from angel investors, accelerators or seed funds to prove people actually want the product (product-market fit). |
| Series A | Typically $10M–$30M from a venture firm to turn early traction into a repeatable business. |
| Series B | Scaling once the model works: hiring fast and growing revenue. Often where a company passes $1B (a unicorn). |
| Series C | Funds market leadership: new products, expansion and acquisitions. New kinds of investors begin to appear. |
| Series D and later | Keeps fuelling growth; companies worth $10B+ are sometimes called decacorns. Rounds can come months apart when growth is fast. |
| Late stage / crossover | Crossover funds, which buy both private and public stocks, join to help prepare for an IPO. |
| Sovereign / mega-round | At very large sizes, sovereign wealth funds (government investment arms) become major backers. |
| Strategic investment | A big company invests because the startup's success helps its own business, often partly paid in services such as cloud computing. |
| Debt / credit facility | Borrowed money that doesn't dilute owners but must be repaid; common for mature companies with steady revenue. |
| Down round | Raising at a lower valuation than before; it dilutes existing owners more and usually signals a reset. |
| Secondary / tender offer | Existing shares change hands (often employees selling to investors); the company raises no new money, but a price gets set. |
| Pre-IPO round | A final private round that often sets a price benchmark before going public. |
| IPO | Banks underwrite the deal, the company files an S-1 disclosing its finances, pitches investors on a roadshow, sets a price and lists on an exchange. |
| Direct listing | Shares start trading on an exchange without the company selling new ones. |
| SPAC merger | The company merges with an already-public shell company to become public. |
| Acquisition | A larger company buys it; this is the most common successful exit for startups. |
| Shutdown | The company winds down; investors usually recover little or nothing. |
